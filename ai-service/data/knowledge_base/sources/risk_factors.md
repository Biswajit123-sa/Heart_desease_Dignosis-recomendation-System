# Heart Disease Risk Factors

## Age and Sex
Cardiovascular risk increases with age. Men generally face elevated risk
earlier in life, while risk in women rises notably after menopause due to
hormonal changes affecting cholesterol and blood vessel health.

## Smoking
Smoking damages blood vessel walls, raises blood pressure, reduces oxygen
in the blood, and significantly increases the likelihood of plaque buildup
in the arteries (atherosclerosis). Smokers have roughly two to four times
the risk of developing coronary heart disease compared to non-smokers.

## Diabetes
High blood sugar over time damages blood vessels and the nerves that
control the heart. Adults with diabetes are at substantially higher risk
of heart disease and tend to develop it at a younger age than people
without diabetes.

## Hypertension (High Blood Pressure)
Persistently high blood pressure forces the heart to work harder, leading
to thickening of the heart muscle and stiffening of the arteries. It is
one of the leading contributors to heart attack and stroke risk.

## High Cholesterol
Elevated LDL ("bad") cholesterol contributes to fatty deposits in artery
walls, narrowing them and restricting blood flow. Low HDL ("good")
cholesterol reduces the body's ability to clear excess cholesterol.

## Obesity
Excess body weight is associated with higher blood pressure, unfavorable
cholesterol levels, and increased risk of type 2 diabetes — all of which
compound cardiovascular risk.

## Family History
A family history of early heart disease (in a father or brother before
age 55, or a mother or sister before age 65) raises individual risk,
suggesting a genetic or shared-environment component.

## Physical Inactivity
Lack of regular physical activity contributes to obesity, high blood
pressure, poor cholesterol profiles, and reduced cardiovascular fitness,
all of which elevate heart disease risk.

## Chest Pain (Angina)
Chest pain or discomfort, especially during exertion, can signal that the
heart muscle is not getting enough oxygen-rich blood, often due to
narrowed coronary arteries. It should always be evaluated by a clinician.
